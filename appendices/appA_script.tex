\section{Invocation Script Reference}

This appendix provides the four‑line Invocation of Rights in English and Spanish, along with concise usage guidance. The script is spoken exactly as written—short for rehearsal, clear for protection. It is a civic tool, not individualized legal advice.

\subsection*{Invocation of Rights (English)}
\begin{quote}
``I invoke my right to remain silent.''\\
``I invoke my right to a lawyer.''\\
``I don’t consent to any searches.''\\
``I want to leave. Am I free to go?''
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Invocación de Derechos (Español)}
\begin{quote}
``Invoco mi derecho a guardar silencio.''\\
``Invoco mi derecho a un abogado.''\\
``No doy permiso para que me registren.''\\
``Quiero irme. ¿Estoy libre para irme?''
\end{quote}

\subsection*{How to use the script}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{Speak calmly and clearly.}\\
      Volume and composure help ensure the words are audible on body‑worn cameras.

\item \textbf{Recite all four lines together.}\\
      Do not add, omit, or remix wording; consistency preserves clarity.

\item \textbf{Repeat if circumstances change.}\\
      If you are formally taken into custody—handcuffed, told you are under arrest, or moved to an interrogation room—recite the same four lines again.  
      The renewed request for a lawyer then triggers the rule that police must stop questioning (\textit{Edwards v.\ Arizona} (1981)).

\item \textbf{Remain silent afterward.}\\
      Answer no questions until counsel is present, unless required to state identifying information by local law.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Delivery tip (optional)}
\begin{quote}
``Hello, officer. I was taught to say this:''
\end{quote}
Some trainers find that opening with this brief preface lowers perceived hostility. It frames the script as a civic routine rather than a personal challenge. The preface is optional and carries no legal effect; it must be followed immediately by the four mandatory lines.
